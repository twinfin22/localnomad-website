# Anti-AI Checklist — Full Reference

Based on X-Poster original methodology. This is a **checklist pass/fail system**, NOT a scoring rubric.

## Banned Structures

### 1. Rule-of-Three
**Pattern**: "X, Y, and Z" used as rhetorical flourish (not actual list of 3 items)
**Example**: "This visa offers flexibility, opportunity, and freedom"
**Fix**: Pick the most specific one. "This visa lets you switch employers."

### 2. Negative Parallelisms (all variants)
**Pattern**: Hedge followed by assertion, in any form
**Variants**:
- "Not just X but Y" → "This isn't just a visa — it's a gateway"
- "Not merely X but Y" → "Not merely a document, but a lifeline"
- "Not only X but also Y" → "Not only does it save time, but it also..."
- "More than just X" → "More than just a visa program"
**Fix**: Just assert directly. "This visa changes everything about how you work here."

### 3. Meta-Commentary
**Pattern**: Talking about the article inside the article
**Examples**: "In this article, we will explore..." / "Let's take a closer look at..." / "As we've discussed above..."
**Fix**: Delete entirely. Just write the content.

### 4. Excessive Transitions
**Pattern**: Over-reliance on transition words between paragraphs
**Examples**: "Furthermore," "Moreover," "Additionally," "In addition," "On top of that,"
**Fix**: Vary connection methods — use context, contrast, or just start the next point. Max 2 transition words per post.

### 5. Hollow Superlatives
**Pattern**: Vague intensifiers without substance
**Examples**: "truly remarkable," "incredibly important," "absolutely essential"
**Fix**: Be specific about WHY it's remarkable. "The processing time dropped from 90 days to 14."

### 6. Fake Authority & Vague Attributions
**Pattern**: Appealing to unnamed experts, studies, or unspecified groups
**Examples**: "Experts agree that..." / "Studies have shown..." / "Research indicates..." / "Scholars suggest..." / "Critics have noted..." / "Many believe..." / "It is widely regarded as..." / "Observers have pointed out..."
**Fix**: Name the expert, cite the study, or remove the claim. Never attribute opinions to unnamed collectives.

### 7. "In conclusion/summary"
**Pattern**: Explicitly announcing the ending
**Examples**: "In conclusion," "To sum up," "In summary," "All in all,"
**Fix**: Delete. Just write the closing thought directly.

### 8. Elegant Variation (Synonym Cycling)
**Pattern**: Avoiding reuse of the same word by cycling through increasingly obscure synonyms for the same thing
**Examples**: Referring to "K-STAR" as "the program," "the initiative," "the pathway," "the scheme," "the framework" all in the same post instead of just calling it "K-STAR" again
**Fix**: Reuse the original term. Repetition is fine — forced synonym rotation is worse. If you must vary, use max 1-2 natural alternatives, not 4-5 obscure ones.

### 9. Em Dash Overuse
**Pattern**: Using em dashes (—) excessively in a formulaic, "punched up" sales-like way
**Examples**: "K-STAR — the fastest path to residency — changes everything" / "The program offers flexibility — and that flexibility — is what sets it apart"
**Threshold**: Max 3 em dashes per 500 words. Beyond that → replace with commas, parentheses, colons, or periods.
**Fix**: Use commas for mild pauses, parentheses for asides, colons for explanations. Save em dashes for genuine dramatic effect.

### 10. Significance/Legacy/Impact Inflation
**Pattern**: Inflating a subject's importance with vague grand claims
**Examples**: "This program has had a lasting impact on..." / "The broader significance of K-STAR..." / "Its cultural legacy continues to shape..." / "This represents a pivotal shift in..."
**Fix**: State the specific, measurable effect. "300 people got F-2 visas through K-STAR since 2023" — not "K-STAR has had a transformative impact on immigration."

### 11. "Challenges and Future Prospects" Pattern
**Pattern**: Formulaic conclusion structure: "Despite its [positives], [subject] faces challenges including..."
**Examples**: "Despite its promising start, K-STAR faces several challenges..." / "While the program has shown success, questions remain about its long-term viability..."
**Fix**: If there are real problems, name them specifically. Don't use the "despite... challenges..." template.

### 12. Promotional/Advertisement-Like Language
**Pattern**: Writing about a subject as if selling it — breathless enthusiasm, feature-listing, superlative stacking
**Examples**: "K-STAR is a groundbreaking, first-of-its-kind program that offers unparalleled opportunities..." / "This innovative visa pathway provides a seamless experience for talented professionals..."
**Fix**: Report factually. Let readers form their own opinions. State what it does, not how amazing it is.

### 13. Chatbot Language Remnants
**Pattern**: Phrases from AI chatbot conversational mode leaking into content
**Examples**: "I hope this helps!" / "Let me know if you have questions" / "Here is a detailed breakdown of..." / "Of course!" / "Certainly!" / "You're absolutely right"
**Fix**: Delete entirely. Blog posts don't talk to the reader like a chatbot.

## Structural Checks

### Check 1: Varied Section Openings
- Read the first sentence of each H2 section
- They must NOT all start with the same structure
- BAD: "The E-7 visa is..." / "The D-8 visa is..." / "The F-1-D visa is..."
- GOOD: "I almost gave up." / "Here's where it gets weird." / "The D-8 visa is..."

### Check 2: Varied Paragraph Length
- Mix of 1-sentence paragraphs, 2-sentence, 3-4 sentence
- NOT all paragraphs the same length
- At least 2 single-sentence paragraphs in the post (Fragment-as-Paragraph, LibaD pattern)

### Check 3: Non-Definition Opening
- The very first sentence of the post must NOT be a definition or general statement
- BAD: "The E-7 visa is a work visa for foreign professionals in Korea."
- GOOD: "I was sitting in the immigration office when the officer said something I didn't expect."

### Check 4: No "In conclusion"
- Search for: "In conclusion," "In summary," "To sum up," "All in all," "To summarize"
- If found → delete and rewrite the closing naturally

### Check 5: Non-Repetitive Headers
- H2 headers must NOT follow a single pattern
- BAD: "What is E-7?" / "What is D-8?" / "What is F-1-D?"
- GOOD: "What is E-7?" / "The D-8 Alternative" / "Why I Chose F-1-D Instead"

### Check 6: Conversational Tone
- Count informal/conversational elements: contractions (don't, can't, it's), fragments, parenthetical asides, direct address ("you"), rhetorical questions
- Must have ≥3 such elements in the post

### Check 7: Opinion Present
- At least 1 clear opinion or judgment expressed
- NOT just neutral reporting of facts
- Example: "Honestly, the D-8 process is broken." / "This is the best-kept secret in Korean immigration."

### Check 8: No Synonym Cycling
- For each key entity (visa name, program name, country), check how many different synonyms are used
- BAD: "K-STAR" → "the program" → "the initiative" → "the pathway" → "the scheme" (5 variants)
- GOOD: "K-STAR" → "the program" (2 variants, fine)
- Rule: Max 2 natural alternatives per key entity. Beyond that = elegant variation.

### Check 9: Em Dash Density
- Count all em dashes (—) in the post
- Threshold: ≤3 per 500 words
- If over threshold → replace excess with commas, parentheses, colons, or periods
- Note: This is a density check, not a ban. Em dashes are fine in moderation.

### Check 10: No "Despite... Challenges" Template
- Search for formulaic "Despite its [positive], [subject] faces challenges" pattern
- Also flag: "While promising, questions remain..." / "Although successful, it is not without..."
- If found → rewrite with specific, concrete observations instead of the template

## Replacement Strategy

When replacing banned words or rewriting banned structures, follow these principles:

1. **Specific**: Replace vague with concrete. "Important" → explain WHY it matters.
2. **Short**: Replacements should be shorter than originals when possible.
3. **Opinionated**: It's OK to have a point of view. "Good" → "the best option if you're self-employed"
4. **Varied rhythm**: Don't replace every banned word with the same alternative. Use different replacements.
5. **Concrete**: Abstract → tangible. "Opportunities" → "3 visa categories that let you work legally"
6. **Repeat, don't rotate**: If you need to mention K-STAR again, just say "K-STAR." Don't cycle through "the program," "the initiative," "the pathway." Repetition beats synonym cycling.
7. **Punctuate simply**: Prefer commas and periods over em dashes. Save em dashes for 1-2 genuine dramatic moments per section, not every other sentence.
