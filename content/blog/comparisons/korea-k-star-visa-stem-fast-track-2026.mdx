You finish your master's in AI at KAIST. Your classmate from the same lab gets a job offer in Silicon Valley. You want to stay in Korea.

The standard path? Get an E-7 work visa. Survive the point system. Wait 6 years. Maybe get permanent residency. Maybe not.

Or: your university president writes one recommendation letter, and you walk out with an F-2 resident visa on graduation day. Three years later, you apply for F-5 permanent residency.

That's K-STAR.

**TL;DR**: K-STAR (Korea Scholarship for Talented Research) gives STEM master's and PhD graduates from 32 approved Korean universities a direct F-2 visa on graduation — no job offer needed, no point system. After 3 years on F-2, you can apply for F-5 permanent residency. About 300 people have used it since the 2023 pilot. Korea plans to push that to 400–600 per year by 2026.

---

## What K-STAR Actually Is (And Isn't)

K-STAR started in 2023 as a pilot with 5 universities — KAIST, GIST, UNIST, DGIST, and UST. The idea was simple: Korea keeps losing its best STEM graduates to the US, Europe, and Singapore. The country's birth rate hit [0.72 in 2023](https://www.korea.net/NewsFocus/Society/view?articleId=267262) (lowest in the OECD — it ticked up to 0.75 in 2024, but still dead last), and its semiconductor and AI sectors can't grow without people.

So the Ministry of Justice created a shortcut. Graduate with a STEM master's or PhD from an approved university, get your president's recommendation, and receive an F-2 resident visa immediately. No job hunt. No employer sponsorship. No grinding through the [E-7 point system](/en/korea/visa/e-7).

In December 2025, the program expanded from 5 to 32 universities nationwide ([Korea Times, Feb 2026](https://www.koreatimes.co.kr/southkorea/k-universities/20260210/korea-to-speed-up-path-to-permanent-residency-for-stem-students)). The government expects annual intake to jump from roughly 100 to 400–600 graduates.

K-STAR is not a separate visa category. It's a fast-track *pathway* into the existing [F-2 resident visa](/en/korea/visa/f-2). Think of it as a VIP lane, not a new highway.

<Callout type="info">
**K-STAR is NOT:**

- A work visa (it's a residency visa — you can work, study, or start a business)
- Open to all foreign graduates (STEM fields only, approved universities only)
- Something you apply for yourself (your university president must recommend you)
- Available for bachelor's degree holders (master's or PhD only, as of March 2026)
</Callout>

The biggest difference isn't time. It's *who decides*. On the standard path, an immigration officer evaluates you against a point system — your salary, Korean language score, tax history, age, all weighted and scored. Thousands of E-7 holders compete for limited F-2-7 slots every year, and many get rejected.

K-STAR moves that decision to your university president. If they recommend you, immigration processes it. The point system doesn't apply.

For context on the broader E-7 pathway and its requirements, see our [Korea E-7 visa guide](/en/korea/visa/e-7).

## Who's Eligible — The 3 Boxes

Your degree needs to check all three. Miss one, and you're back on the standard path.

**Box 1: Degree level** — Master's or PhD. No exceptions as of March 2026. Bachelor's degree holders are not eligible, and there's no indication this will change soon.

**Box 2: University** — One of 32 approved K-STAR universities in Korea. The confirmed list includes KAIST, GIST (Gwangju Institute of Science and Technology), UNIST (Ulsan National Institute of Science and Technology), DGIST (Daegu Gyeongbuk Institute of Science and Technology), UST (University of Science and Technology), and Seoul National University. The remaining universities have been selected but the full public list is still being released as of March 2026. Your international student office can confirm whether your university participates.

**Box 3: Field** — STEM only. Specifically:
- Artificial intelligence and machine learning
- Semiconductor engineering
- Biotechnology and life sciences
- Robotics and automation
- Quantum computing
- Data science
- Advanced materials science
- Related disciplines (contact your university for edge cases)

The recommendation itself comes from your university president — you don't apply to immigration directly. This means your academic standing and research contribution matter, but the exact criteria for who gets recommended varies by institution.

- The complete list of all 32 universities (only ~6 publicly confirmed)

- Whether you must remain physically in Korea during the 3-year F-2 period to qualify for F-5
- Performance review requirements for the F-5 application after 3 years
- Family sponsorship timeline and specific conditions
- Whether the program will expand to bachelor's degree holders

## Why Korea Built This

Korea isn't doing this out of generosity. It's doing this out of fear.

The country's population is shrinking. Birth rates have been below replacement since the 1980s, and the 2023 figure of 0.72 children per woman made Korea the fastest-declining population among developed nations (it nudged up to 0.75 in 2024 — still last place by a wide margin). Over 80 municipalities are now designated "depopulation zones" (인구소멸위험지역).

Meanwhile, STEM talent flows out. A PhD candidate at KAIST finishes their dissertation and gets offers from Google Brain, a Zurich research lab, and a Korean chipmaker. The Korean offer pays less, requires a complicated visa process, and locks you to one employer. The US offer comes with a relocation package and an H-1B pathway that (eventually) leads to a green card.

K-STAR is Korea's counter-offer: stay here, and we'll give you residency faster than any other country in the region. No job lock. No point system. Three years to permanent residency.

It's part of a [broader 2026 immigration overhaul](/en/blog/news/korea-2026-immigration-overhaul) that also created the Top-Tier Visa for senior researchers and the K-Core visa for skilled manufacturing workers. Three different programs, three different talent pools, one shared motivation: Korea needs people.

## FAQ

**Can bachelor's degree holders apply for K-STAR?**
Not as of March 2026. The program is limited to master's and PhD graduates. There has been no public indication that this will change, but the program is still expanding and future updates are possible.

**Do I need to find a job before getting the K-STAR F-2 visa?**
No. That's the main difference from the standard E-7 path. Your university president recommends you, and you receive F-2 status on graduation. You can then work for any employer, freelance, or start a business.

**What happens if my university isn't on the approved list?**
You'd follow the standard E-7 → F-2 → F-5 pathway. Check with your university's international student office first — the list expanded to 32 universities in late 2025 and your school may have been added.

K-STAR is still young. About 300 people have gone through it since 2023, and major details remain unfinished. But the signal is clear: if you're studying STEM in Korea and want to stay long-term, this is the fastest legal pathway to permanent residency available anywhere in East Asia.

Check with your university's international student office. If your school is on the list, start the conversation about the president's recommendation before you graduate — not after.
