## F-1-D at a Glance: East Asia's Longest DN Visas

Korea's [F-1-D Workation visa](/en/korea/visa/f-1-d) is one of the longest digital nomad visas in East Asia. [Japan](/en/japan/visa/digital-nomad-jp) caps you at 6 months (and it's non-renewable). [Taiwan](/en/taiwan/visa/gold-card)'s newer option gives 2 years. Korea's F-1-D? **Up to 2 years (1 year initial, renewable once in-country).** That's a real win if you want to stay long-term without reapplying from abroad.

**Here's why Korea created this:** The government recognized that foreign remote workers spend money locally (housing, food, coworking, entertainment) without competing for Korean jobs. The F-1-D is Korea's bet that long-stay remote workers help the domestic economy. It's working. Thousands of digital nomads, freelancers, and remote employees now hold this visa.

**Why this matters:** If you're planning an extended Northeast Asia workation, Korea is now a serious contender alongside [Japan](/en/blog/comparisons/japan-korea-taiwan-digital-nomad-visa-2026) and [Taiwan](/en/blog/comparisons/japan-korea-taiwan-digital-nomad-visa-2026).

| Visa | Duration | Renewal | Income Req | Best For |
|------|----------|---------|-----------|----------|
| **Korea F-1-D** | 1-2 years | In-country (1+1) | ₩88M (~$64K) | Long-stay remote workers |
| **Japan DN Visa** | 6 months | Not renewable | ¥10M (~$68K) | Short-term visitors |
| **Taiwan DN Visa** | 2 years | Not renewable | $40K (age 30+) | Serious long-term stays |

## 2026 Eligibility Requirements

Korea updates the F-1-D income threshold annually based on Gross National Income (GNI) per capita. As of 2026, these are the official requirements:

### Income Threshold: 2× GNI Per Capita (~₩88 Million)

The core requirement: You must prove **annual income of at least twice the previous year's GNI per capita as announced by the Bank of Korea** ([source: Ministry of Justice](https://www.moj.go.kr/bbs/immigration/47/580178/artclView.do) | [English PDF](https://www.immigration.go.kr/bbs/immigration_eng/229/464290/download.do)). Based on 2024 GNI of ₩44,051,000, that's currently ~₩88 million. The USD equivalent depends on the exchange rate on the day you apply, roughly $64,000–66,000 at recent rates. This threshold updates annually, typically each April.

**Important:** This is gross income, not net. If you earn $64K/year from freelancing or remote employment, you qualify. If you earn $55K, you don't. Not yet. The threshold is firm.

### How to Prove Income (Required Documents)

Korea's immigration office accepts multiple document types. You need **at least one**, but having multiple strengthens your application:

- **Tax certificates** (preferred): Previous year's tax return or officially issued income certificate from your home country's tax authority
- **Employment letter**: Signed contract from your foreign employer stating annual salary, position, and employment dates
- **Client contracts**: If freelancing, contracts from clients (foreign companies only) showing project scope, payment terms, and annual projected income
- **Bank statements**: 12 months of statements showing consistent deposits matching claimed income
- **Combination approach**: Mix multiple documents. Example: tax certificate + employment letter + bank statements = strongest case

**Timeline tip:** If your 2025 income is borderline, you can use projections or contracts for 2026 income if they're signed and official.

### Work Experience: Minimum 1 Year with Foreign Employer

You must have **at least 1 year of work experience** with a **non-Korean employer** (any country except South Korea). This rule excludes people who've only worked domestically in Korea.

**What counts:**
- Remote employment with any overseas company (US, UK, Canada, Australia, etc.)
- Freelancing for international clients (as long as they're non-Korean entities)
- Self-employment as a digital service provider serving foreign markets

**What doesn't count:**
- Work for Korean companies (even if done remotely from abroad)
- Domestic Korean freelance work

### Health Insurance: ₩100M (~$74–77K) Minimum Coverage

You must have **valid health and medical repatriation insurance** with **minimum ₩100 million (~$74,000–77,000 USD depending on exchange rate) coverage**. This is mandatory for visa issuance.

**How to get it:**
- International travel insurance providers (Allianz, AXA, Cigna, IMG) all cover Korea
- After arrival, consider buying local Korean coverage, which is cheaper than international plans
- Insurance must be valid for your entire F-1-D period (1-2 years)
- International insurers typically charge $600-1,200/year for adequate coverage

**Pro tip:** Buy a 2-year international policy before applying if you're certain about staying 2 years. It's cheaper than annual renewals and simplifies reapplication.

## New in 2026: In-Country Status Change

This is the biggest change. As of early 2026, **visa-free visitors to Korea can now convert to F-1-D status without leaving the country**. Previously, you had to apply at an embassy/consulate before arriving. This change massively simplifies the process. (Honestly, it's the update that makes Korea worth considering over Japan and Taiwan for most people.)

### Who Can Use In-Country Conversion

- B-1/B-2 visa-free visitors (most Western nationals: US, UK, Canada, Australia, EU, etc.)
- Currently in Korea on visa-free entry status
- Meeting all F-1-D eligibility criteria (income, insurance, work experience)

### Which Immigration Office Handles Conversion

**Seoul Immigration Service** (강남구) is the primary processing center, but major cities now have capacity:
- **Seoul**: Gangnam-gu office (largest, fastest processing)
- **Busan**: Busan Immigration Office
- **Incheon**: Incheon Immigration Office
- **Daegu**: Regional office (slower; use Seoul if possible)

**Recommendation:** Apply at Seoul Gangnam office even if you're based elsewhere. It's the fastest and most experienced with F-1-D applications.

### Documents Needed for In-Country Conversion

1. **Completed application form**: Form No. 34 / 통합신청서 (Integrated Application Form)
2. **Passport**: Original + copies of all pages
3. **Income proof**: Tax certificate, employment letter, contracts (see section above)
4. **Health insurance certificate**: Proof of ₩100M+ coverage (English or Korean translation)
5. **Work experience verification**: Letter from employer or client contract
6. **Bank statements**: Last 12 months showing income deposits
7. **Accommodation proof**: Rental contract, hotel booking, or residence verification
8. **Visa application fee**: ₩60,000 (~$45 USD)

### Processing Timeline: 2-4 Weeks

Expect **14-28 days for approval** once you submit documents. The office will contact you via phone or email with the result.

**Timeline example:**
- Day 1: Submit application at immigration office
- Days 3-7: Officer reviews documents, may request clarifications
- Days 10-14: Background check completed
- Days 14-28: Final approval and ARC (residence permit card) issuance
- Day 28+: Collect your ARC

**Faster processing tip:** Submit a complete application with all documents on day one. Incomplete applications add 1-2 weeks.

## Step-by-Step Application Process

### Starting from Outside Korea (Embassy/Consulate Route)

If you're outside Korea, apply through a Korean embassy or consulate in your home country.

**1. Contact your nearest Korean embassy/consulate**
- Website lookup: mofa.go.kr (search "visa application")
- Most require appointment; some have online booking
- Processing time: 5-10 business days typically

**2. Prepare required documents**
- Completed visa application form (Form No. 17 / 사증발급신청서)
- Passport (valid 6+ months beyond visa date)
- Income documentation (tax certificate, employment letter, etc.)
- Health insurance proof (₩100M+ coverage)
- Recent photograph (3.5x4.5 cm, white background)
- Bank statements (12 months)
- Work experience letter

**3. Submit in person**
- Most embassies require in-person submission
- Some accept mailed applications (check locally)
- Fee: ₩60,000 (~$45 USD) or equivalent in local currency

**4. Collect visa**
- Single-entry workation visa in your passport
- Valid 1 year (renewable in Korea, see "Renewal" section below)
- Collect in person or by mail (depends on embassy)

**Timeline:** 2-3 weeks total (including appointment wait time)

### Already in Korea? Convert Your Status In-Country ✨ New for 2026

This is now the preferred option if you can get to Korea visa-free first.

**1. Enter Korea visa-free**
- Eligible citizens (US, UK, Canada, EU, etc.) get 90-180 days visa-free depending on nationality
- Arrive as tourist; no special documents needed
- You have entire visa-free period to gather documents and apply

**2. Schedule appointment at Seoul Immigration (Gangnam)**
- Website: immigration.go.kr → "Workation Visa" → "In-country application"
- Online appointment booking available
- Walk-ins possible but expect 2-3 hour waits

**3. Prepare documents** (same list as overseas route above)

**4. Submit at immigration office**
- In-country application fee: ₩60,000
- All documents reviewed same-day
- Officer may ask clarification questions

**5. Collect approval and ARC**
- ARC (Alien Registration Card) issued after approval (2-4 weeks)
- ARC = residence permit; required for banking, housing, etc.
- Collect at immigration office or designated police station

**Timeline:** 3-5 weeks total (including visa-free entry + processing)

### Complete Document Checklist

**Financial Documents:**
- [ ] Previous year tax return or income certificate
- [ ] Employment letter signed by employer (on company letterhead)
- [ ] Client contracts (if freelancing) showing annual value
- [ ] 12 months bank statements showing income deposits
- [ ] Health insurance policy document with ₩100M+ coverage confirmation

**Personal Documents:**
- [ ] Valid passport (6+ months validity after visa date)
- [ ] 3.5x4.5 cm photograph (white background, recent)
- [ ] Work experience letter from current/previous employer
- [ ] Accommodation proof (rental lease, hotel booking, Airbnb confirmation)

**Application Documents:**
- [ ] Completed visa application form (Form No. 17 for overseas, Form No. 34 for in-country)
- [ ] Visa application fee (₩60,000 cash or card)

**Optional but recommended:**
- [ ] Cover letter explaining your work and why Korea
- [ ] Proof of ties to Korea (if applicable: friends, family, prior stays)

## The Tax Trap: 183 Days and Korean Tax Residency

If you spend **183 or more days in Korea in a calendar year**, you become a Korean tax resident and owe income tax on worldwide earnings (6% to 45% progressive). Korea has 97 double taxation treaties that usually prevent paying twice, but you still need to file.

The short version: stay under 183 days or budget for a 세무사 (tax accountant, ₩500K-1.5M). Read the full breakdown in our [183-day tax trap guide](/en/blog/tips/183-day-tax-trap-digital-nomads).

## What You Cannot Do on F-1-D

The F-1-D is strictly for remote work with foreign employers/clients. Korea enforces these restrictions seriously.

### Prohibited Activities

**Local employment:** Cannot work for Korean companies or organizations, even part-time or unpaid (volunteering counts)

**Korean clients/freelancing:** Cannot freelance for Korean entities or individuals, even if paid by foreign intermediaries

**Korean business registration:** Cannot start or register a Korean company, sole proprietorship, or LLC

**Physical presence requirement:** Must be physically present in Korea (cannot be on "visa" while working from another country)

### Enforcement & Penalties

**How Korea enforces:**
- Random workplace audits (immigration officers visit companies)
- Whistleblower reports from co-workers
- Employer audits by tax authority (세무청)
- Border/airport checks comparing departure records with work visa status

**Penalties if caught:**
- **Immediate visa cancellation**: F-1-D revoked, must leave within 14 days
- **Work permit suspension**: Cannot reapply for F-1-D for 1-2 years
- **Employer fines**: Korean company faces ₩10-50M fines for employing visa violators
- **Tax penalties**: Back taxes, interest, and 20-40% penalty if income wasn't declared

**Real example:** A developer on F-1-D took contract work with a Korean startup (paid by startup to his US company account). Immigration audited the startup, found his name in internal systems, and canceled his visa. He lost F-1-D status and had to leave Korea immediately.

**Golden rule:** If the work benefits a Korean entity directly, don't do it on F-1-D.

## Living in Korea on F-1-D

Once your visa is approved, the real challenge starts: banking, phone, housing, and healthcare. F-1-D holders face a catch-22 where most services require an ARC (residence card), but the ARC takes 2-4 weeks after approval.

Key things to know: **Woori Bank** is the most reliable for F-1-D accounts. Buy a **prepaid SIM at the airport** on arrival. Go with **월세 (monthly rent)** over 전세 for 1-2 year stays. NHIS kicks in after 6 months, so carry private insurance until then.

## Renewal: 1+1 Extension (The Hidden Lifeline)

One of F-1-D's best features: **you can renew in-country for another year**. This wasn't always the case; many visa types require you to leave Korea and reapply from outside. F-1-D is different.

### How In-Country Renewal Works

After your initial 1-year F-1-D expires, you can extend directly at immigration.

**Requirements for renewal (same as initial):**
- Income proof for the past year (current tax documents, employment letter for new period)
- Health insurance (must remain valid through renewal period)
- Proof of residence (updated rental contract)
- Clean record (no visa violations, criminal activity, or work violations)
- ARC still valid (must apply before expiration)

**Timeline:**
- **Apply 30-60 days before current visa expires** (immigration prefers early application)
- Processing: 2-4 weeks typically
- Fee: ₩60,000

**Maximum total stay:** Two 1-year periods = 2 years total on F-1-D. After 2 years, you must leave Korea or switch to a different visa type (F-2, marriage visa, etc.).

**Pro tip:** Start planning your 2-year exit or visa transition at the 18-month mark. Don't wait until the last minute.

## Common F-1-D Mistakes to Avoid

Learn from others' missteps:

**Mistake 1: Waiting until the last minute to apply**
- Don't wait until your tourist visa expires to start F-1-D application
- Start application 60+ days before visa-free entry expires
- Too-last-minute submissions get rejected and you get deported

**Mistake 2: Underestimating income documentation**
- "I'll explain my income verbally" doesn't work
- No tax certificate? Get an accountant to write a verified income letter (비인증소득확인서)
- Vague employment letters fail; insist on detailed, official letters from employers

**Mistake 3: Taking just-barely-qualifying work**
- "I'll start a Korean freelance side gig"? Illegal on F-1-D, visa gets canceled
- Stick exclusively to foreign clients to be safe

**Mistake 4: Forgetting the 183-day rule**
- Staying 185 days and surprised by Korean tax bill. Easily ₩5M+ if undeclared
- Count your days; leave before day 183 or be prepared to file taxes

**Mistake 5: Not setting up health insurance early**
- Waiting until month 6 for NHIS when you could have private insurance month 1
- Medical emergency costs ₩5M-20M without insurance
- Buy international insurance immediately; switch to NHIS when eligible

**Mistake 6: Choosing wrong bank**
- "I'll use Shinhan Bank because it's popular"? Shinhan often rejects F-1-D
- Verify with Woori Bank or KEB Hana first; call ahead

## FAQ: Common F-1-D Questions

**Q: Can I work for a Korean company if they're paying me via foreign account?**
A: No. If the work benefits a Korean entity, it's violation. Immigration audits Korean companies and finds your name in systems. Visa gets canceled. Stick to exclusively foreign clients.

**Q: Does the F-1-D allow me to teach English?**
A: No. Teaching (even private tutoring for Korean students) requires E-2 visa. F-1-D is remote-work-only.

**Q: What if my income drops below ₩88M during the year?**
A: For renewal, you need to prove ₩88M again. If income drops mid-year, you don't immediately lose visa. But renewal will be denied. Plan accordingly.

**Q: Can my spouse/partner apply for F-1-D too?**
A: Yes, if they meet requirements independently (₩88M income, 1+ year work experience, insurance). No spousal dependency; separate applications required.

**Q: What happens if I overstay my F-1-D?**
A: Immigration will fine you ₩100,000+ per day overstay; ban you from re-entering Korea for 1-10 years depending on length of overstay. Don't overstay.

**Q: Can I convert F-1-D to work visa (E-7) if I get hired by Korean company?**
A: Yes, but you need sponsor (the Korean company). Company must file for E-7 visa; you submit ARC for conversion. Costs ₩100K and takes 1-2 weeks. F-1-D terminates; new visa status begins.

**Q: What if I don't make ₩88M but I have ₩50M in savings?**
A: Savings don't count for income requirement. It must be declared income (from work). Having ₩50M in the bank is great for living expenses, but won't qualify you for F-1-D.

**Q: Can I study Korean on F-1-D?**
A: Yes. Casual language classes, university courses, etc. are fine. The restriction is employment. If you're studying full-time and working part-time, verify with immigration.

**Q: Will the 183-day tax rule apply if I leave for 1 week to Thailand?**
A: No. If you leave Korea and stay outside for 1+ consecutive days, that day doesn't count toward 183. But some authorities interpret "physically present" strictly. If you're traveling frequently (in/out), consult tax accountant.

**Q: What's the best visa to start with: F-1-D or F-2 (long-term resident)?**
A: F-2 requires independent qualification (employment, family ties, property, or points-based). F-1-D is easier to get initially, but does not convert directly to F-2. You must independently meet F-2 requirements and apply separately.

## Renewal & Beyond: Planning Your 2-Year Timeline

Since F-1-D maxes out at 2 years (1 year initial + 1 year renewal), plan your long-term Korea strategy now.

### Year 1 Exit Options (If You Don't Renew)

- **Move to another country**: Japan, Taiwan, Southeast Asia
- **Apply for different visa**: F-2 (long-term), E-7 (employment), Marriage visa (if applicable)
- **Take a break**: Leave Korea for 2-3 months, return as tourist, then re-apply for F-1-D

### Year 2 Renewal (If You Renew for Final Year)

- **Re-apply at immigration**: 30-60 days before expiration
- **Must prove income again**: ₩88M minimum
- **Insurance required**: Must stay valid through renewal period
- **Same restrictions apply**: No Korean employment, foreign clients only

### Beyond Year 2

After 2 years of F-1-D, you must switch visa types. Options:

| Visa Type | Duration | Requirements | Best For |
|-----------|----------|--------------|----------|
| **F-2 Long-Term Resident** | 2 years (renewable indefinitely) | Points system (80+/170): income, education, age, Korean ability | Long-term stayers |
| **E-7 Employment Visa** | 2 years (renewable) | Korean company job sponsorship | Employees |
| **Marriage Visa** | 3 years (renewable) | Married to Korean citizen | Married people |
| **Student Visa (D-2)** | 2-5 years | University enrollment | Students |

**Recommendation:** At 18-month mark, start exploring which visa type suits your long-term plans. If you want to stay 5+ years, start building ties toward F-2 or employment visa now.

## Final Thoughts: Is F-1-D Right for You?

The F-1-D Workation visa isn't perfect. It has quirks:
- Banking is tedious
- Tax residency rules are strict
- You can't work for Koreans
- It maxes out at 2 years

But for remote workers earning $64K+/year who want to stay 12-24 months in Korea without constant visa-hopping, **the F-1-D is genuinely the best option in East Asia right now.**

Japan's 6-month limit means you're always renewing. Taiwan's DN Visa is excellent but 2-year no-renewal means less flexibility. Korea's F-1-D gives you the longest flexibility: try 1 year, renew if it works, leave if not.

**If you're serious about Korea, F-1-D unlocks 2 years to truly settle in, build community, and experience Seoul beyond tourist mode.**
