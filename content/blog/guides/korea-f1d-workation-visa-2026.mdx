## F-1-D at a Glance — Among East Asia's Longest DN Visas

Korea's [F-1-D Workation visa](/en/korea/visa/f-1-d) is one of the longest digital nomad visas in East Asia. [Japan](/en/japan/visa/digital-nomad-jp) caps you at 6 months (and it's non-renewable). [Taiwan](/en/taiwan/visa/gold-card)'s newer option gives 2 years. Korea's F-1-D? **Up to 2 years (1 year initial, renewable once in-country).** That's a real win if you want to stay long-term without reapplying from abroad.

**Here's why Korea created this:** The government recognized that foreign remote workers spend money locally (housing, food, coworking, entertainment) without competing for Korean jobs. The F-1-D is Korea's bet that long-stay remote workers help the domestic economy. It's working—thousands of digital nomads, freelancers, and remote employees now hold this visa.

**Why this matters:** If you're planning an extended Northeast Asia workation, Korea is now a serious contender alongside [Japan](/en/blog/comparisons/japan-korea-taiwan-digital-nomad-visa-2026) and [Taiwan](/en/blog/comparisons/japan-korea-taiwan-digital-nomad-visa-2026).

| Visa | Duration | Renewal | Income Req | Best For |
|------|----------|---------|-----------|----------|
| **Korea F-1-D** | 1-2 years | In-country (1+1) | ₩100M (~$70K) | Long-stay remote workers |
| **Japan DN Visa** | 6 months | Not renewable | ¥10M (~$68K) | Short-term visitors |
| **Taiwan DN Visa** | 2 years | Not renewable | $40K (age 30+) | Serious long-term stays |

## 2026 Eligibility Requirements

Korea updates the F-1-D income threshold annually based on Gross National Income (GNI) per capita. As of 2026, these are the official requirements:

### Income Threshold: ₩100 Million (~$70,000 USD)

The core requirement: You must prove **annual income of at least ₩100 million (approximately $70,000 USD at current exchange rates)**. This is doubled Korea's GNI per capita, updated yearly on April 1st based on the previous year's GNI.

**Important:** This is gross income, not net. If you earn $70K/year from freelancing or remote employment, you qualify. If you earn $60K, you don't—yet. The threshold is firm.

### How to Prove Income (Required Documents)

Korea's immigration office accepts multiple document types. You need **at least one**, but having multiple strengthens your application:

- **Tax certificates** (preferred) — Previous year's tax return or officially issued income certificate from your home country's tax authority
- **Employment letter** — Signed contract from your foreign employer stating annual salary, position, and employment dates
- **Client contracts** — If freelancing, contracts from clients (foreign companies only) showing project scope, payment terms, and annual projected income
- **Bank statements** — 12 months of statements showing consistent deposits matching claimed income
- **Combination approach** — Mix multiple documents. Example: tax certificate + employment letter + bank statements = strongest case

**Timeline tip:** If your 2025 income is borderline, you can use projections or contracts for 2026 income if they're signed and official.

### Work Experience: Minimum 1 Year with Foreign Employer

You must have **at least 1 year of work experience** with a **non-Korean employer** (any country except South Korea). This rule excludes people who've only worked domestically in Korea.

**What counts:**
- Remote employment with any overseas company (US, UK, Canada, Australia, etc.)
- Freelancing for international clients (as long as they're non-Korean entities)
- Self-employment as a digital service provider serving foreign markets

**What doesn't count:**
- Work for Korean companies (even if done remotely from abroad)
- Domestic Korean freelance work

### Health Insurance: ₩100M (~$75,000) Minimum Coverage

You must have **valid health and medical repatriation insurance** with **minimum ₩100 million ($75,000 USD) coverage**. This is mandatory for visa issuance.

**How to get it:**
- **International travel insurance providers** — Allianz, AXA, Cigna, IMG (all cover Korea)
- **Korean insurance** — After arrival, consider buying local coverage (cheaper than international)
- **Coverage timeline** — Insurance must be valid for your entire F-1-D period (1-2 years)
- **Cost estimate** — International insurers typically charge $600-1,200/year for adequate coverage

**Pro tip:** Buy a 2-year international policy before applying if you're certain about staying 2 years. It's cheaper than annual renewals and simplifies reapplication.

## New in 2026 — In-Country Status Change

This is the biggest change. As of early 2026, **visa-free visitors to Korea can now convert to F-1-D status without leaving the country**. Previously, you had to apply at an embassy/consulate before arriving. This change massively simplifies the process.

### Who Can Use In-Country Conversion

- B-1/B-2 visa-free visitors (most Western nationals: US, UK, Canada, Australia, EU, etc.)
- Currently in Korea on visa-free entry status
- Meeting all F-1-D eligibility criteria (income, insurance, work experience)

### Which Immigration Office Handles Conversion

**Seoul Immigration Service** (강남구) is the primary processing center, but major cities now have capacity:
- **Seoul** — Gangnam-gu office (largest, fastest processing)
- **Busan** — Busan Immigration Office
- **Incheon** — Incheon Immigration Office
- **Daegu** — Regional office (slower, use Seoul if possible)

**Recommendation:** Apply at Seoul Gangnam office even if you're based elsewhere. It's the fastest and most experienced with F-1-D applications.

### Documents Needed for In-Country Conversion

1. **Completed application form** — Form No. 34 / 통합신청서 (Integrated Application Form)
2. **Passport** — Original + copies of all pages
3. **Income proof** — Tax certificate, employment letter, contracts (see section above)
4. **Health insurance certificate** — Proof of ₩100M+ coverage (English or Korean translation)
5. **Work experience verification** — Letter from employer or client contract
6. **Bank statements** — Last 12 months showing income deposits
7. **Accommodation proof** — Rental contract, hotel booking, or residence verification
8. **Visa application fee** — ₩100,000 (~$75 USD)

### Processing Timeline: 2-4 Weeks

Expect **14-28 days for approval** once you submit documents. The office will contact you via phone or email with the result.

**Timeline example:**
- Day 1: Submit application at immigration office
- Days 3-7: Officer reviews documents, may request clarifications
- Days 10-14: Background check completed
- Days 14-28: Final approval and ARC (residence permit card) issuance
- Day 28+: Collect your ARC

**Faster processing tip:** Submit a complete application with all documents on day one. Incomplete applications add 1-2 weeks.

## Step-by-Step Application Process

### Option A: Applying from Overseas (Embassy/Consulate Route)

If you're outside Korea, apply through a Korean embassy or consulate in your home country.

**1. Contact your nearest Korean embassy/consulate**
- Website lookup: mofa.go.kr (search "visa application")
- Most require appointment; some have online booking
- Processing time: 5-10 business days typically

**2. Prepare required documents**
- Completed visa application form (Form No. 17 / 사증발급신청서)
- Passport (valid 6+ months beyond visa date)
- Income documentation (tax certificate, employment letter, etc.)
- Health insurance proof (₩100M+ coverage)
- Recent photograph (3.5x4.5 cm, white background)
- Bank statements (12 months)
- Work experience letter

**3. Submit in person**
- Most embassies require in-person submission
- Some accept mailed applications (check locally)
- Fee: ₩100,000 (~$75 USD) or equivalent in local currency

**4. Collect visa**
- Single-entry workation visa in your passport
- Valid 1 year (renewable in Korea, see "Renewal" section below)
- Collect in person or by mail (depends on embassy)

**Timeline:** 2-3 weeks total (including appointment wait time)

### Option B: Applying In-Country (Immigration Office Route) ✨ New for 2026

This is now the preferred option if you can get to Korea visa-free first.

**1. Enter Korea visa-free**
- Eligible citizens (US, UK, Canada, EU, etc.) get 90-180 days visa-free depending on nationality
- Arrive as tourist; no special documents needed
- You have entire visa-free period to gather documents and apply

**2. Schedule appointment at Seoul Immigration (Gangnam)**
- Website: immigration.go.kr → "Workation Visa" → "In-country application"
- Online appointment booking available
- Walk-ins possible but expect 2-3 hour waits

**3. Prepare documents** (same list as "Option A" above)

**4. Submit at immigration office**
- In-country application fee: ₩100,000
- All documents reviewed same-day
- Officer may ask clarification questions

**5. Collect approval and ARC**
- ARC (Alien Registration Card) issued after approval (2-4 weeks)
- ARC = residence permit; required for banking, housing, etc.
- Collect at immigration office or designated police station

**Timeline:** 3-5 weeks total (including visa-free entry + processing)

**Financial Documents:**
- [ ] Previous year tax return or income certificate

- [ ] Client contracts (if freelancing) showing annual value

**Optional but recommended:**

## The Tax Trap — 183 Days and Korean Tax Residency

This is the section nobody tells you about until it's too late. If you stay too long, Korean tax laws catch you. See [our 183-day tax trap guide](/en/blog/tips/183-day-tax-trap-digital-nomads) for deeper tax strategy and country-specific treaty information.

### The 183-Day Rule: Becoming a Korean Tax Resident

If you spend **183 or more days in Korea in a single calendar year**, you automatically become a **Korean tax resident**. This has major implications.

**What this means:**
- You owe **Korean income tax on worldwide income**—not just money earned in Korea, but ALL your remote work income from every client and employer globally
- Korean tax residents are taxed progressively: **6% to 45% depending on income bracket**
- This is mandatory; the F-1-D visa does not exempt you
- It applies equally to all visa types (tourist, F-1-D, F-2, etc.)

**Timeline example:** If you arrive January 1 and stay until July 22, that's 183 days exactly. You are a Korean tax resident for the entire calendar year and must file taxes on all 2026 worldwide income.

### Korean Income Tax Rates (2026)

Korea uses a progressive tax system. As a tax resident, you owe taxes on worldwide income at these rates:

| Annual Income (KRW) | Tax Rate |
|-------------------|----------|
| Up to ₩14M | 6% |
| ₩14M - ₩50M | 15% |
| ₩50M - ₩88M | 24% |
| ₩88M - ₩150M | 35% |
| ₩150M - ₩300M | 38% |
| ₩300M - ₩500M | 40% |
| ₩500M - ₩1B | 42% |
| Over ₩1B | 45% |

**Example:** If you earn ₩100M ($75K), your tax breakdown is:
- First ₩14M × 6% = ₩840K
- Next ₩36M × 15% = ₩5.4M
- Next ₩38M × 24% = ₩9.12M
- Last ₩12M × 35% = ₩4.2M
- **Total tax: ~₩19.56M (19.6% effective rate)**

### Double Taxation Treaties: Your Lifeline

Korea has signed **90+ double taxation treaties** with most countries, including the US, UK, Canada, Australia, and major European nations. These treaties prevent you from being taxed twice on the same income.

**How they work:**
1. You earn $70K from US clients (taxable in the US)
2. You become a Korean tax resident (also taxable in Korea)
3. The treaty allows you to claim a **foreign tax credit** in Korea
4. You pay taxes in your home country (higher rate) and pay **zero in Korea** (because of the credit)

**Action required:**
- File your US/UK/Canadian taxes normally
- When filing Korean taxes, claim the foreign tax credit
- Consult a Korea-based tax accountant (licensed 세무사) to ensure proper filing
- Estimated cost: ₩500K-1.5M ($375-1,125 USD) for accounting services

**Critical:** Different treaties have different rules. US citizens (FATCA) have stricter reporting. British citizens (different treaty) may have different credits. **Always consult a professional.**

### Practical Example: US Citizen on F-1-D Staying 200 Days

You're a US citizen earning $70K/year from a US tech company. You arrive in Korea January 1 and stay until July 19 (200 days).

**What happens:**
1. **US taxes** — You file Form 1040 as usual, pay US federal tax (~$8,000 at 12% average rate)
2. **Korean taxes** — You file Korean income tax return, showing ₩88M income (~$70K)
3. **Double tax treaty** — File Form 9465 (Foreign Tax Credit claim) with US IRS
4. **Result** — US taxes paid exceed Korean taxes owed, so you claim credit for overpayment; likely get refund or carry forward credit

**Timeline for taxes:**
- End of calendar year (Dec 31): Tax year ends
- April 15 (next year): US tax return due
- May 31 (next year): Korean tax return due
- Plan ahead if staying multiple years

### Strategy: Tracking Your Days Carefully

If you're borderline on the 183-day rule, you need precision.

**Days count as:**
- Arrival date = Day 1 (even if you arrive at midnight)
- Departure date = Last day in Korea
- Days outside Korea don't count (but you must leave the country, not just stay in your apartment)
- Visa-free entry = Counts toward 183 days

**Pro strategy for avoiding tax residency:**
- Stay 182 days max in Korea (leave on Day 182)
- Go to Japan, Taiwan, or Southeast Asia for 3-4 weeks
- Return to Korea for another 182-day stint (different calendar year)
- You never trigger the 183-day rule in a single calendar year

**Risk:** This strategy is legal but flagged by tax authorities. Don't abuse it (authorities monitor visa extensions and border crossings).

**Better strategy:** If you're planning to stay 6+ months, consult a Korean tax accountant before arrival. Discuss:
- Business entity structure (should you incorporate in Korea or stay independent?)
- Tax treaty benefits specific to your country
- Whether self-incorporation saves taxes

## What You Cannot Do on F-1-D

The F-1-D is strictly for remote work with foreign employers/clients. Korea enforces these restrictions seriously.

### Prohibited Activities

**Local employment:** Cannot work for Korean companies or organizations, even part-time or unpaid (volunteering counts)

**Korean clients/freelancing:** Cannot freelance for Korean entities or individuals, even if paid by foreign intermediaries

**Korean business registration:** Cannot start or register a Korean company, sole proprietorship, or LLC

**Physical presence requirement:** Must be physically present in Korea (cannot be on "visa" while working from another country)

### Enforcement & Penalties

**How Korea enforces:**
- Random workplace audits (immigration officers visit companies)
- Whistleblower reports from co-workers
- Employer audits by tax authority (세무청)
- Border/airport checks comparing departure records with work visa status

**Penalties if caught:**
- **Immediate visa cancellation** — F-1-D revoked, must leave within 14 days
- **Work permit suspension** — Cannot reapply for F-1-D for 1-2 years
- **Employer fines** — Korean company faces ₩10-50M fines for employing visa violators
- **Tax penalties** — Back taxes, interest, and 20-40% penalty if income wasn't declared

**Real example:** A developer on F-1-D took contract work with a Korean startup (paid by startup to his US company account). Immigration audited the startup, found his name in internal systems, and canceled his visa. He lost F-1-D status and had to leave Korea immediately.

**Golden rule:** If the work benefits a Korean entity directly, don't do it on F-1-D.

### Banking: Which Banks Accept F-1-D Holders

This used to be a major pain point. As of 2026, it's improved but still limited.

**Banks that accept F-1-D:**
- **Woori Bank** — Most reliable; explicitly supports F-1-D (check your local branch)
- **KB Kookmin Bank** — Some branches accept; inconsistency by location
- **KEB Hana Bank** — Recently started accepting; verify before applying
- **Foreign banks** — Citibank Korea, HSBC (if available; more expensive, fewer ATMs)

**Banks that don't accept F-1-D:**
- **NH Bank** — Strict policy against short-term visa types
- **Shinhan Bank** — Generally requires work visa (employment visa type)

**Application requirements:**
- Passport + ARC (residence permit card)
- Proof of address (rental contract, hotel booking)
- Source of income documentation (employment letter)
- Initial deposit (usually ₩100,000 minimum)

**Timeline:** 1-2 visits; same-day account opening possible

**Pro tip:** Visit bank branches in Gangnam-gu, Seoul (expat hub) or near universities (more used to foreigners). Rural branches often refuse.

### Phone Plans: Prepaid USIM Options

Korean phone plans require local verification. F-1-D holders have limited options.

**Best option: Prepaid plans**
- **SKT, KT, LG U+** (three major carriers) all offer prepaid "tourist" plans
- Valid 1-12 months; no long-term contract needed
- Cost: ₩25,000-50,000/month depending on data
- Data: Unlimited 4G/5G available; Korea's network is world-class

**Where to buy:**
- Airport convenience stores (easiest; immediate activation)
- GS25, CU, Lotte Mart convenience stores (nationwide)
- Carrier website (online activation, mail SIM to accommodation)
- Tourist information centers

**Alternative: eSIM**
- Some carriers offer digital eSIM (growing but limited availability)
- No physical card; instant activation
- Check your phone's compatibility first

**Recommendation:** Buy at airport on arrival. Takes 10 minutes; you'll have phone immediately.

### Housing: Understanding Jeonse & Wolse

Korean housing terminology confuses foreign renters. Here's the breakdown.

**Wolse (월세)** — Monthly rent (most common for foreigners)
- Pay monthly rent directly to landlord
- Deposit typically ₩5-10M (refundable at lease end)
- Similar to Western monthly rent
- Cost: ₩500K-1.5M/month in Seoul for 1BR depending on neighborhood

**Jeonse (전세)** — Lump sum deposit instead of monthly rent
- Pay large upfront deposit (typically 50-70% of property value); landlord holds it for lease term
- Pay ₩0 monthly rent
- When lease ends, landlord returns full deposit
- Example: 2-year lease on ₩300M apartment = you pay ₩200M deposit, ₩0 monthly, get ₩200M back after 2 years
- **Advantage:** Cheaper long-term (no monthly bleeding)
- **Disadvantage:** Requires massive upfront capital; risky (landlord could default on return)
- Mainly used by long-term expats and Korean citizens

**Best for F-1-D:** Wolse (monthly rent) is practical for 1-2 year stays. Jeonse requires trust in landlord and capital you might not have.

### When You Get Your ARC (Residence Permit Card)

The ARC (외국인등록증) is your residence permit and ID.

**Issued:** After F-1-D visa approval (typically 2-4 weeks into Korea)
**Validity:** Matches your visa (1-2 years)
**Uses:**
- Required for opening bank accounts
- Proof of residence (housing, utilities)
- Employee ID with some employers
- Proof of legal status at police/immigration

**How to get it:**
- Go to nearest immigration office or police station after visa approval
- Bring notification letter + passport
- Takes 1-2 visits
- Cost: ₩30,000

**Pro tip:** Get this ASAP after arriving. Many services require ARC immediately.

### Healthcare: NHIS Enrollment Timeline for F-1-D Holders

Korea's National Health Insurance (NHIS) is excellent and mandatory for residents.

**Timeline for F-1-D:**
- **Months 1-6:** Private insurance required (not eligible for NHIS yet)
- **Month 6+:** Eligible to apply for NHIS
- **Month 6-7:** NHIS enrollment typically approved
- **Cost:** Approximately 6.5% of reported income; employees pay half (employer pays half if employed by Korean company)

**How to enroll:**
1. Visit National Health Insurance Corporation (건강보험공단) office with ARC + income documentation
2. Declare monthly income for contribution calculation
3. Pay first month's premium (~₩150K-300K depending on income)
4. Receive insurance card and coverage (effective immediately in many cases)

**Coverage includes:**
- Outpatient visits (30% copay typical)
- Inpatient hospitalization (20% copay typical)
- Dental care (limited coverage; 30-50% copay)
- Prescription drugs
- No US-style deductibles

**Pro tip:** Before month 4, buy international travel health insurance (₩500K-1.2M upfront for 6-month coverage). Once NHIS kicks in, you can switch and get refund for unused portion.

**Cost comparison:**
- International insurance: ~$600-1,200/year
- NHIS: ~₩1.2M-2.4M/year (6.5% of income)
- Example: If you declare ₩100M income, NHIS costs ~₩6.5M/year = better than international insurance

## Renewal: 1+1 Extension (The Hidden Lifeline)

One of F-1-D's best features: **you can renew in-country for another year**. This wasn't always the case; many visa types require you to leave Korea and reapply from outside. F-1-D is different.

### How In-Country Renewal Works

After your initial 1-year F-1-D expires, you can extend directly at immigration.

**Requirements for renewal (same as initial):**
- Income proof for the past year (current tax documents, employment letter for new period)
- Health insurance (must remain valid through renewal period)
- Proof of residence (updated rental contract)
- Clean record (no visa violations, criminal activity, or work violations)
- ARC still valid (must apply before expiration)

**Timeline:**
- **Apply 30-60 days before current visa expires** (immigration prefers early application)
- Processing: 2-4 weeks typically
- Fee: ₩100,000

**Maximum total stay:** Two 1-year renewals = 2 years total on F-1-D. After 2 years, you must leave Korea or switch to different visa type (F-2, marriage visa, etc.).

**Pro tip:** Start planning your 2-year exit or visa transition at the 18-month mark. Don't wait until the last minute.

## Visa Comparison: F-1-D vs Japan DN vs Taiwan Gold Card

Here's the real comparison that matters:

| Factor | **Korea F-1-D** | **Japan DN Visa** | **Taiwan DN Visa** |

### Which Visa Should You Pick?

- **F-1-D:** Pick this if you want to stay in Korea 1-2 years and might renew. Best for serious Korea-based digital nomads.
- **Japan DN:** Pick this if you want a low-income threshold and short 6-month trial. Not for long-term.
- **Taiwan DN:** Pick this if you want a 2-year guarantee without renewal stress. Taiwan's banking is easier; healthcare faster. Best for non-committal 2-year explorers.

## Common F-1-D Mistakes to Avoid

Learn from others' missteps:

**Mistake 1: Waiting until the last minute to apply**
- Don't wait until your tourist visa expires to start F-1-D application
- Start application 60+ days before visa-free entry expires
- Too-last-minute submissions get rejected and you get deported

**Mistake 2: Underestimating income documentation**
- "I'll explain my income verbally"—doesn't work
- No tax certificate? Get an accountant to write a verified income letter (비인증소득확인서)
- Vague employment letters fail; insist on detailed, official letters from employers

**Mistake 3: Taking just-barely-qualifying work**
- "I'll start a Korean freelance side gig"—illegal on F-1-D, visa gets canceled
- Stick exclusively to foreign clients to be safe

**Mistake 4: Forgetting the 183-day rule**
- Staying 185 days and surprised by Korean tax bill—easily ₩5M+ if undeclared
- Count your days; leave before day 183 or be prepared to file taxes

**Mistake 5: Not setting up health insurance early**
- Waiting until month 6 for NHIS when you could have private insurance month 1
- Medical emergency costs ₩5M-20M without insurance
- Buy international insurance immediately; switch to NHIS when eligible

**Mistake 6: Choosing wrong bank**
- "I'll use Shinhan Bank because it's popular"—Shinhan often rejects F-1-D
- Verify with Woori Bank or KEB Hana first; call ahead

## Dealing with Korean Bureaucracy: Survival Tips

Korean government processes can feel overwhelming. Here's how to navigate:

### Key Government Offices You'll Visit

1. **Immigration Service (출입국관리사무소)**
   - Handle: Visa applications, ARC (residence permit), visa extensions
   - Locations: Every city; Seoul Gangnam-gu is main hub (busiest)
   - Hours: Usually 9 AM-5 PM, closed Sundays/holidays
   - Pro tip: Go early (8:30 AM) to get low number; appointment system often broken

2. **National Health Insurance Corporation (건강보험공단)**
   - Handle: NHIS enrollment, premium payments
   - Location: Every district (Gu); find at nhis.or.kr
   - Hours: Usually 9 AM-6 PM, closed Saturdays/Sundays

3. **Police Station (경찰서 or 파출소)**
   - Handle: Alien registration, residence verification
   - Location: Every neighborhood; tiny booths (파출소) everywhere
   - Hours: 24 hours (desk staff 9 AM-6 PM; emergency 24/7)

4. **Gu Office (구청) — District Administrative Office**
   - Handle: Residence registration, tax matters, general admin
   - Location: Every district
   - Hours: 9 AM-6 PM, closed weekends

### Language Barrier Solutions

- **Interpreter service:** Most offices have English-speaking staff OR appointment slots reserved for English speakers (call ahead)
- **Google Translate:** Download offline; decent for simple bureaucratic Korean
- **Kakao Talk translation:** Built-in; photo translation works for forms
- **Hire helper:** For ₩50-100K, immigration lawyers or "visa expediters" (비자대행업체) can handle paperwork (legal gray area; use cautiously)
- **Buddy system:** Find Korean friend or expat with Korean skills to accompany you

**Golden rule:** Never assume an office will speak English. Call ahead; ask for English-speaking staff or time slots reserved for foreigners.

## FAQ: Common F-1-D Questions

**Q: Can I work for a Korean company if they're paying me via foreign account?**
A: No. If the work benefits a Korean entity, it's violation. Immigration audits Korean companies and finds your name in systems. Visa gets canceled. Stick to exclusively foreign clients.

**Q: Does the F-1-D allow me to teach English?**
A: No. Teaching (even private tutoring for Korean students) requires E-2 visa. F-1-D is remote-work-only.

**Q: What if my income drops below ₩100M during the year?**
A: For renewal, you need to prove ₩100M again. If income drops mid-year, you don't immediately lose visa. But renewal will be denied. Plan accordingly.

**Q: Can my spouse/partner apply for F-1-D too?**
A: Yes, if they meet requirements independently (₩100M income, 1+ year work experience, insurance). No spousal dependency; separate applications required.

**Q: What happens if I overstay my F-1-D?**
A: Immigration will fine you ₩100,000+ per day overstay; ban you from re-entering Korea for 1-10 years depending on length of overstay. Don't overstay.

**Q: Can I convert F-1-D to work visa (E-7) if I get hired by Korean company?**
A: Yes, but you need sponsor (the Korean company). Company must file for E-7 visa; you submit ARC for conversion. Costs ₩100K and takes 1-2 weeks. F-1-D terminates; new visa status begins.

**Q: What if I don't make ₩100M but I have ₩50M in savings?**
A: Savings don't count for income requirement. It must be declared income (from work). Having ₩50M in the bank is great for living expenses, but won't qualify you for F-1-D.

**Q: Can I study Korean on F-1-D?**
A: Yes. Casual language classes, university courses, etc. are fine. The restriction is employment. If you're studying full-time and working part-time, verify with immigration.

**Q: Will the 183-day tax rule apply if I leave for 1 week to Thailand?**
A: No. If you leave Korea and stay outside for 1+ consecutive days, that day doesn't count toward 183. But some authorities interpret "physically present" strictly. If you're traveling frequently (in/out), consult tax accountant.

**Q: What's the best visa to start with: F-1-D or F-2 (long-term resident)?**
A: F-2 requires independent qualification (employment, family ties, property, or points-based). F-1-D is easier to get initially, but does not convert directly to F-2. You must independently meet F-2 requirements and apply separately.

## Taxes Redux: Deep Dive for the Anxious

If you're still worried about taxes, let me clarify further.

### Scenario 1: You Stay 180 Days (SAFE)

- Not a Korean tax resident (under 183-day threshold)
- No Korean income tax filing required
- Still file taxes in your home country on worldwide income
- You owe nothing to Korea

### Scenario 2: You Stay 190 Days (TAXABLE)

- You ARE a Korean tax resident for the full calendar year
- You owe Korean tax on worldwide income earned in that year
- Example: Jan 1–Jul 8 = 190 days in Korea
- You earned $70K in 2026 (Jan-Dec) from US clients
- You owe Korean tax on ₩88M equivalent (~$70K)
- Using double taxation treaty, you likely owe $0 Korea tax (because US taxes exceed Korea's rate)
- You DO have to file (even if it results in $0 payment)

### Scenario 3: You Stay 200 Days in Year 1, Then 150 Days in Year 2

- Year 1: Tax resident; must file Korean taxes
- Year 2: NOT a tax resident (under 183 days); no Korean taxes
- But: Work income earned in Year 2 still taxable in home country
- Total tax bill: Year 1 (Korean filing required; likely $0 due to treaty) + Year 2 (home country only)

### How to File Korean Taxes if You're Tax Resident

1. **Hire accountant (세무사)** — Cost ₩500K-1.5M ($375-1,125)
2. **Gather documents:**
   - Prior year income (tax return, invoices, contracts)
   - Bank statements showing income deposits
   - Employment letters from foreign clients/employers
3. **Accountant files on your behalf** — May 31 deadline
4. **Pay any amount owed** — Usually $0 if treaty applies; could be ₩500K-3M if no treaty

**Best practice:** Find English-speaking accountant NOW (before you need them). Cost ₩100K for consultation. Worth it.

## Final Thoughts: Is F-1-D Right for You?

The F-1-D Workation visa isn't perfect. It has quirks:
- Banking is tedious
- Tax residency rules are strict
- You can't work for Koreans
- It maxes out at 2 years

But for remote workers earning $70K+/year who want to stay 12-24 months in Korea without constant visa-hopping, **the F-1-D is genuinely the best option in East Asia right now.**

Japan's 6-month limit means you're always renewing. Taiwan's DN Visa is excellent but 2-year no-renewal means less flexibility. Korea's F-1-D gives you the longest flexibility: try 1 year, renew if it works, leave if not.

**If you're serious about Korea, F-1-D unlocks 2 years to truly settle in, build community, and experience Seoul beyond tourist mode.**
