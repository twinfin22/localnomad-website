## What Is NPS, and Why Is It Taking Your Money?

Korea's 국민연금 (National Pension Service, NPS) is mandatory social insurance. If you work for a Korean employer — full-time, part-time, on a contract — they enroll you automatically. There's no opt-out.

The rate in 2026 is **9.5% of your gross salary**. That splits evenly: you pay 4.75% through paycheck deductions, your employer pays another 4.75% directly to NPS. You never see the employer half on your payslip, but it exists and it accumulates alongside yours.

For Korean nationals, this makes sense. They retire here. For foreigners on a 2-year teaching contract or a D-8 startup visa, it can feel like a mandatory savings account you can't touch.

The good news: Korea lets you take all of it back when you leave. Both halves. Plus interest accrued over your contribution period. This is genuinely unusual — most countries with social insurance systems either don't let foreigners withdraw at all or only return the employee share.

The refund is called the **탈퇴일시금** (withdrawal lump-sum). If your home country has a social security agreement with Korea, you might have additional options. But even without one, most foreigners qualify for at least partial return.

## Who Can Claim? Countries, Visas, and Agreements

Eligibility comes through three separate tracks. You only need to qualify under one.

### Track 1: Social Security Agreement Countries (24 countries)

Korea has signed bilateral Social Security Agreements (SSA) with 24 countries. Citizens of these countries are eligible for the lump-sum refund regardless of visa type:

<ResponsiveTable>
| Region | Countries |
|---|---|
| Americas | USA, Canada (including Quebec's separate QPP agreement), Brazil, Uruguay, Chile |
| Europe | Germany, UK, France, Austria, Belgium, Netherlands, Czech Republic, Hungary, Poland, Slovakia, Romania, Bulgaria, Croatia |
| Asia-Pacific | Japan, China (mainland), Australia, India, Thailand |
| Other | Turkey |
</ResponsiveTable>

SSA agreements are added periodically. Check NPS directly if your country isn't listed — it may have been added recently.

### Track 2: Reciprocity Countries (~25 countries)

Even without a formal SSA, Korea extends refund eligibility to citizens of countries that extend equivalent rights to Korean nationals under their own pension systems. This list overlaps with Track 1 but includes additional countries across Southeast Asia, the Middle East, and parts of Africa. It changes as bilateral arrangements are updated.

If your country isn't on the SSA list, ask NPS whether your nationality qualifies under reciprocity (상호주의). The answer is often yes.

### Track 3: E-8, E-9, and H-2 Visa Holders (Always Eligible)

This track bypasses nationality entirely. Holders of E-8 (non-professional employment), E-9 (non-professional employment — manufacturing, agriculture, fisheries), and H-2 (working holiday — ethnic Korean) visas are always eligible for the lump-sum refund, regardless of which country they're from.

This was built specifically for migrant workers from countries with no pension agreement with Korea. The intent was practical: workers in these visa categories are unlikely to ever collect a Korean pension, so a clean exit mechanism makes more sense.

### USA Special Case: Totalization or Lump-Sum

American citizens have two paths. Under the Korea-USA totalization agreement, you can combine Korean and US work years toward eligibility for a monthly pension in either country (requires 10+ combined years). If you don't meet that threshold, you take the lump-sum refund instead. Most short-to-mid-term workers end up taking the lump-sum.

### If None of These Apply

Some nationalities fall outside all three tracks. In that case, your NPS contributions are not refundable as a lump-sum departure benefit. Contact NPS to confirm before assuming the worst — the eligible country list expands periodically.

## How Much Will You Get Back?

The formula is simple. The exact number depends on your salary history, contribution months, and the year you enrolled:

**Refund = (Salary × 9.5% × Months Contributed) + Interest — Withholding Tax**

A worked example:

<ResponsiveTable>
| Item | Amount |
|---|---|
| Monthly salary | ₩3,500,000 |
| Contribution period | 24 months |
| Total contributions (9.5%) | ₩7,980,000 |
| Interest accrued (approx.) | ₩240,000 |
| Gross refund | ₩8,220,000 |
| Tax withheld (퇴직소득세, ~2%) | ₩164,400 |
| **Net refund** | **≈ ₩8,055,600** |
</ResponsiveTable>

Real numbers will differ based on your salary history (NPS uses monthly reported income, which may differ from actual take-home if your employer reported differently) and the exact interest calculation NPS applies.

### The Tax Withholding: Variable, Not Fixed

The 퇴직소득세 (retirement income tax) withheld at the time of refund is **not a flat rate**. It scales with your total refund amount and years of service, and Korea applies tax treaty rates for eligible nationalities.

For most foreigners who worked 1-5 years in Korea and receive a moderate refund, the effective withholding ends up somewhere in the **1-3% range**. Higher earners or longer-tenure workers may see slightly more. The specific rate depends on your tax residency situation and whether Korea's double-tax treaty with your home country applies.

You may be able to reclaim some or all of this withholding when you file taxes in your home country. See [Korea double-tax treaty rules](/en/blog/tax/korea-double-tax-treaty-guide-2026) for how this works by country.

## Method 1: Cash at Incheon Airport (Same Day)

This is the most dramatic option. You can walk into Incheon Airport on the day of your flight and walk out with cash in hand (in one of 16 foreign currencies). Here is how:

**Step 1: Visit your local NPS office before your last week in Korea.**
Request a Certificate of Eligibility for Lump-Sum Refund (탈퇴일시금 지급청구서). Confirm that your employer has reported your resignation or contract end to NPS — if they haven't filed the paperwork, processing gets complicated fast.

**Step 2: Gather your documents.**
You'll need: passport, alien registration card (or visa page if ARC was already returned), pension contribution statements (your employer should have given you these, or NPS can print them), and the certificate from Step 1.

**Step 3: Arrive at Terminal 1, 1F, between Exits 1 and 2.**
The NPS airport service counter is at **Booths 7-8**, operated by Woori Bank. Hours are **09:00-18:00, weekdays only**. There is no weekend service. There is no late-night service.

**Step 4: Submit your claim at the NPS/Woori Bank counter.**
The staff will verify your documents and process the refund claim on the spot.

**Step 5: Receive your cash in foreign currency.**
Woori Bank converts the refund from KRW into your chosen currency. 16 options available — major currencies including USD, EUR, GBP, JPY, AUD, CAD, and several others. You leave with cash.

**Step 6: Board your flight.**
The airport center handles flights departing Terminal 1 from **10:30 to 24:00**, so there's a window where you can arrive, claim, and still make your flight. Check-in times matter — don't cut it close.

<Callout type="warning">
If you're departing from Terminal 2 (Korean Air, Delta, Air France, KLM, and others), you must still go to Terminal 1 for the NPS airport center. T2 has no NPS counter. Build in at least 30-40 extra minutes for the transit between terminals. T2 departures: the service window is 11:00-24:00.
</Callout>

The airport option is popular for a reason. You get cash, you don't have to wait weeks, and it's a clean break. The downside: you need everything ready before you arrive, the hours are limited, and weekends don't work.

## Method 2: Bank Transfer After Departure

The quieter option. Less dramatic, more forgiving if you forgot to prepare.

You apply directly through NPS, either before you leave Korea or any time before the 5-year deadline. The claim can be submitted at any NPS office before departure, or through the NPS international contact center after.

Processing takes approximately **4-6 weeks**. NPS transfers the refund in KRW to a foreign bank account you designate. Exchange rate risk is yours — you get whatever the KRW rate is at the time of transfer.

Documents required are similar to the airport method: passport copy, bank account details (SWIFT code, account number, bank address), and the departure stamp on your passport (or proof of address abroad). Some applicants submit via email or fax; others visit the NPS office in person before flying.

This method works well if:
- You're leaving on a weekend and the airport center is closed
- You have a connecting flight through T2 with no time margin
- You simply forgot to sort this out before your last day

The main trade-off is time. You won't see the money for 4-6 weeks, and currency conversion happens on NPS's schedule, not yours.

## Method 3: Claim From Abroad (The "I Forgot" Option)

You're already home. Maybe you've been home for two years and just found out this money exists. This method is for you.

The process involves submitting a notarized claim package by mail to NPS Korea. The package typically includes:
- Completed refund claim form (available on NPS website in English)
- Copy of passport (notarized)
- Copy of departure record or entry stamp in your home country passport
- Bank account details for international wire transfer
- 위임장 (power of attorney) if someone in Korea is filing on your behalf

Some Korean consulates or embassies in major cities will help you process this, particularly in countries with significant Korean expat communities. Worth calling before attempting the mail route on your own.

Timeline for mail claims varies — 6-8 weeks is common, occasionally longer if documents need clarification.

The notarization requirement adds friction and cost (varies by country, typically $20-80 USD for basic notarization). But it beats losing the money entirely.

## Country Spotlight: What to Know Before You Go

**USA**: Eligible under SSA. Can choose totalization (monthly pension if 10+ combined years) or lump-sum. Most people take lump-sum. Tax treaty may reduce or eliminate withholding — check [Korea double-tax treaty rules](/en/blog/tax/korea-double-tax-treaty-guide-2026).

**Canada**: Eligible under SSA. Lump-sum available. Canada has its own pension system (CPP), and refunding Korean NPS doesn't affect CPP contributions. Standard bank transfer is most common.

**UK**: Eligible under SSA. Similar to Canada — clean lump-sum refund, no overlap with UK state pension contributions. Bank transfer works well.

**Australia**: Eligible under SSA. Australia has superannuation (separate from NPS), so returning NPS doesn't create any complications with your super. The airport cash option works if you're organized.

**China (mainland)**: Eligible under SSA since 2013. Processing for Chinese nationals is standard. Some applicants use the mail route given travel logistics.

**Southeast Asia (Vietnam, Philippines, Indonesia, Thailand, etc.)**: Thailand is covered under SSA. Vietnam, Philippines, and Indonesia are not SSA countries but may qualify under reciprocity or if on E-8/E-9/H-2 visas. Check with NPS directly — and if you were on a non-professional work visa, you're almost certainly eligible under Track 3.

One thing that matters for everyone: the [183-day residency rule](/en/blog/tax/183-day-tax-trap-digital-nomads) can affect your Korean tax residency status in the year you depart, which in turn affects how the NPS withholding interacts with your overall tax situation.

## The 5-Year Clock

This is the rule that turns unclaimed refunds into lost money.

Under 국민연금법 §115-116, the right to claim the lump-sum refund expires **5 years from the date you depart Korea** (or 5 years from the end of your last contribution period if you left earlier). After that deadline, the unclaimed funds become part of the general pension reserve. NPS will not notify you. There are no reminders.

The 5-year window sounds long. And then it isn't.

People move back, get busy, forget the account existed. The amounts involved — anywhere from ₩500,000 for a short stay to ₩15,000,000+ for longer tenures — are real money. The employer share alone is money you never held in your hands but earned.

One practical note: if it has been over a year since you left Korea and you haven't filed, start soon. Notarization for the mail method can take weeks to arrange, and processing adds more time. Don't let the 5-year deadline become a close call.

## FAQ

### Can all foreigners get a pension refund from Korea?

Not automatically — eligibility depends on your nationality and visa type. Citizens of SSA countries (24 countries), citizens of reciprocity countries, and all E-8/E-9/H-2 visa holders can claim. If you're unsure which category applies to you, call NPS International at +82-63-713-6900 before your departure date.

### How long does the refund take?

Airport cash: same day (weekdays 09:00-18:00 only). Bank transfer: 4-6 weeks. Mail from abroad: 6-8 weeks, sometimes longer.

### How much tax is withheld from the refund?

The 퇴직소득세 withheld is **variable** — it's calculated based on your total refund amount, years of service, and applicable tax treaty rates between Korea and your home country. For most foreigners working 2-5 years in Korea, the effective rate lands somewhere in the 1-3% range. It is not a fixed rate. NPS will give you the exact figure when you apply. You may be able to reclaim some of this against your home country tax liability.

### Is the USA eligible?

Yes. Under the Korea-USA Social Security Agreement, American citizens can claim the lump-sum refund. If you have 10+ combined US and Korean work years, you may qualify for a monthly pension through totalization instead — but most people take the lump-sum.

### What is the deadline to claim?

5 years from your departure date (or end of contribution period). This is a hard legal deadline under 국민연금법 §115-116. No extensions, no grace period, no exceptions.

### Can I claim without visiting an NPS office in Korea?

Yes. The mail-from-abroad method lets you submit a notarized claim package to NPS without returning to Korea. Some Korean consulates in major cities assist with this process. Processing is slower, but the money is the same.

### What if my employer never paid into NPS on my behalf?

This happens. If your employer didn't report or pay your NPS contributions correctly, your refund will be based on reported contributions only. If you have pay stubs showing deductions that don't match NPS records, you can file a complaint with NPS. This is a separate process and worth pursuing — employers who underreport are liable for the difference plus penalties.
